AI Frgetting

The Intentional and Unintentional Sides of Knowledge Evolution

San Francisco, California•Single-day, in-person•Date to be announced

Workshop Scope

AI systems are becoming long-term participants in human life. The information that shapes them is no longer fixed at deployment: it may be acquired, revised, retrieved, propagated across components, and carried forward over time.

As these systems learn over longer periods, acquiring new knowledge is only half of the problem. Some information should persist because it supports continuity, competence, safety, or useful personalization. Other information may become outdated, sensitive, withdrawn, corrected, or harmful, and should no longer shape the system’s behavior.

Unintentional forgetting is a reliability failure: useful knowledge, capabilities, or safety-relevant behavior disappear even though they should remain. Intentional forgetting is a deliberate control operation: selected examples, identities, concepts, facts, associations, or memories should cease to influence future behavior.

Current research often studies these problems separately. Continual and lifelong learning ask how useful knowledge can be preserved, while machine unlearning asks how specified information can be removed. In persistent assistants, adaptive models, and long-lived agents, however, the boundary between what should remain and what should be forgotten can itself change over time. A correction, data withdrawal, policy update, or revoked private context may also need to propagate through model weights, retrieval stores, summaries, and external memory.

AIF brings these questions together. The workshop connects researchers in continual and lifelong learning, machine unlearning, trustworthy AI, privacy, model auditing, and agent memory to study how knowledge persistence should be specified, changed, and verified across the AI lifecycle.

Central question As AI becomes a long-term participant in human life, what and when should it forget, who should decide, and how can we verify that forgetting is selective, effective, and durable?

Specification

What should persist, be revised, expire, or be removed—and whose interests define that boundary?

Intervention

How can retention, revision, memory control, and machine unlearning carry out the intended change?

Assurance

What evidence shows that forgetting is selective, auditable, resistant to recovery, and durable?

Topics of Interest

  • Unintentional forgetting in lifelong learning: catastrophic forgetting, capability erosion, continual adaptation, test-time drift, and losses caused by fine-tuning, merging, distillation, or compression.
  • Intentional forgetting and selective control: machine unlearning, concept erasure, model editing, certified guarantees, and utility–collateral damage trade-offs.
  • Verification and residual influence: privacy attacks, representation probes, recovery and relearning tests, black-box auditing, and formal or empirical guarantees.
  • Memory in long-lived agents: persistent state, RAG and external memory, expiration and deletion policies, contextual integrity, and reintroduction risks.
  • Mechanisms and theory: knowledge localization, entanglement, sparsity, influence, weight-space editing, stability–plasticity, and the limits of selective control.
  • Specification and lifecycle governance: user intent, provenance, correction, privacy, copyright, licensing, regulation, model retirement, and auditable request interfaces.
  • Durability and evaluation across time: joint measurement of retention and removal, longitudinal tests, later training or memory updates, reproducibility, and negative results.

Call for Papers

We invite original and unpublished technical, empirical, benchmark, theory, systems, governance, and position papers that address AI forgetting in long-lived systems.

Full Paper

Up to 8 pages + references

Original technical, empirical, benchmark, theory, or systems work.

Short Paper

4-page extended abstract

Work in progress, focused findings, position pieces, or recent work included to seed discussion.

Tiny Paper

2 pages + references

Compact ideas, re-analyses, follow-up experiments, modest theoretical observations, or early-stage work.

Non-archival workshop. Acceptance to AIF will not constitute publication or prevent later submission elsewhere. Novel and ongoing work will be prioritized for contributed talks.

Submission Information

Submission platform
All tracks will run through OpenReview. The workshop group and submission link will be posted after workshop acceptance.
Format
Submissions must be a single PDF and use the official ICLR 2027 style. Track-specific page limits are listed above; the official template link will be added when it becomes available.
Double-blind review
Submissions must be anonymized. Author names, affiliations, acknowledgements, and identifying information in supplementary or linked material should be removed for review.
Dual submissions
Concurrent submissions are welcome where the policies of the other venue permit. Previously published work may be considered in the Short Paper track when it directly advances workshop discussion, but AIF is not intended as a second presentation venue for completed conference papers.
LLM usage policy
AIF will follow the official ICLR 2027 policy on large language model usage. AI systems may not serve as primary authors or reviewers, and authors and reviewers remain responsible for their submissions and evaluations. Tiny and short papers must satisfy ICLR’s human-authorship requirements.
Accepted papers
Accepted papers will be publicly visible on OpenReview by the mandatory notification deadline. Camera-ready instructions and presentation assignments will follow during March–April 2027.
Conflicts of interest
Submissions will use OpenReview conflict flags. Organizers will not submit papers to the workshop, and conflicted submissions will be handled only by non-conflicted organizers or external area chairs.
